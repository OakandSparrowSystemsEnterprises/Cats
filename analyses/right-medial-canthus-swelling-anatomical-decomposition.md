# Right medial canthus and nasal bridge swelling in an adult white cat: subcranial anatomical decomposition

Scope: this works only from a written description of photographs produced by another model. No image was seen. Nothing here is a diagnosis of the cat, and no probabilities are assigned. Anatomical statements are general feline and carnivore relationships; where a relationship varies between individual cats, or where my confidence is limited, section 8 says so.

## 1. What the described surface pattern constrains

The description fixes six things:

1. Center of the abnormality: right medial canthus, upper lateral nasal bridge, adjacent dorsal maxillary and infraorbital region.
2. The right palpebral fissure is narrower than the left.
3. The fullness reads as volume arising from beneath the skin, not a discrete superficial skin mass.
4. Dark material or staining at the medial aspect of the right eye.
5. The nasal planum is still midline.
6. No wound, draining tract, protruding mass, or bilateral swelling.

What these constrain anatomically:

- Item 1 places the center of mass over the junction of three bones: the caudal end of the nasal bone, the dorsal (frontal) process of the maxilla, and the lacrimal bone at the rostromedial orbital rim. Directly deep to that junction is the caudal nasal cavity (ethmoidal region) on the medial side and the rostromedial orbit on the lateral side. Nothing else is there. Any process producing volume at this point lives in one of five compartments: the subcutis, the lacrimal apparatus, the nasal cavity pushing outward, the maxilla itself, or the medial orbit pushing forward.
- Item 3 says the skin is being lifted, not replaced. That favors a deeper compartment with an intact or thinned covering and disfavors, without excluding, a cutaneous tumor, which matters in a white cat.
- Item 4 marks the nasolacrimal apparatus as obstructed, irritated, infected, or simply overflowing. On a white coat, chronic tear overflow leaves red-brown porphyrin staining; dried mucopurulent discharge is yellow-brown; dried blood is dark red to black. Any of the three says something is wrong at the medial canthus, along the duct, or at the duct's outlet in the nose.
- Item 5 says the rostral nasal cavity and the septum are not yet displaced. It says nothing about the caudal cavity, which is where the described swelling sits.
- Item 6 says that if this is an infection it has not yet pointed, if a foreign body there is no visible entry, and whatever it is, it is one-sided.

Three things the description does not give, and which move the ranking more than any further reasoning: the texture (bone-hard, firm-rubbery, doughy, fluctuant), the time course (days, weeks, months), and whether the bridge is visibly wider on the right when viewed from directly above. Section 6 lists these with the rest of the bedside observations.

## 2. Outside inward: what lies under the right medial canthus and lateral nasal bridge

### 2.1 Skin and subcutis

The skin over the nasal bridge and medial canthus is thin and tightly adherent. Subcutaneous fat is minimal. Swelling in this layer spreads along fascial planes rather than doming, which is why early cellulitis here reads as fullness rather than as a lump. A bite-wound abscess, the commonest cause of facial swelling in outdoor cats, lives in this layer.

### 2.2 Cutaneous facial muscles and the medial palpebral ligament

Beneath the skin: the orbicularis oculi encircling the fissure, the levator anguli oculi medialis, and the levator nasolabialis, which arises from the frontal and nasal region and runs rostroventrally across the maxilla to the upper lip and nostril. The medial palpebral ligament anchors the medial canthus to the bone at the rostromedial orbital rim. These muscles are a sheet, not a mass. They are the path along which pus from the maxilla tracks dorsally toward the medial canthus.

### 2.3 Medial canthus and the nasolacrimal system

At the medial canthus: the lacrimal caruncle, the base of the third eyelid with its gland (ventromedial to the globe), and the upper and lower lacrimal puncta in the lid margins a few millimeters from the canthus. The puncta lead through short canaliculi to the lacrimal sac, which sits in a shallow fossa in the lacrimal bone at the rostromedial orbital rim, directly deep to the medial canthal skin. From the sac the nasolacrimal duct passes through the lacrimal canal (lacrimal bone, then maxilla) and runs rostroventrally in the lateral wall of the nasal cavity, under the nasal mucosa, to open in the ventral nasal meatus near the nostril. Along that course it passes close to the apex of the maxillary canine root.

Two consequences. Anything that obstructs the duct (a nasal mass, a canine root lesion, a lacrimal sac foreign body, swelling of the lateral nasal wall) produces tear overflow and staining at the medial canthus. An infected lacrimal sac (dacryocystitis) produces a focal swelling exactly at the medial canthus, slightly ventral to it, sometimes with material that can be expressed back out of the puncta, and sometimes a fistula through the skin below the canthus.

### 2.4 Bone at the site

- Nasal bones: paired, thin, forming the dorsum of the bridge, joined at the midline internasal suture; their caudal ends meet the frontal bones.
- Maxilla: forms the lateral wall of the nasal cavity, the lateral face below the nasal bone, the infraorbital canal, the alveolar process holding the upper tooth roots, and part of the hard palate. In the cat the infraorbital canal is very short, and the infraorbital foramen opens on the lateral maxilla roughly dorsal to the third premolar, a short distance rostral to the orbital rim. The cat has no true maxillary sinus; it has a small maxillary recess lateral to the caudal nasal cavity at the level of the rostroventral orbit, above the carnassial roots, housing the lateral nasal gland.
- Lacrimal bone: small, at the rostromedial orbital rim, carrying the lacrimal fossa.
- Frontal bone: forms the forehead, the dorsomedial orbital rim and the dorsomedial orbital wall. It contains the frontal sinus, which in the cat is paired with a larger lateral and a smaller medial compartment on each side, lying dorsal and medial to the orbit and extending caudally over the rostral cranial cavity. Its rostral limit is near the level of the orbit. A frontal sinus lesion presents on the forehead above and behind the eye, not at the medial canthus, unless it extends rostroventrally.
- Ethmoid bone: the ethmoidal labyrinth (ethmoturbinates) fills the caudal nasal cavity directly medial to the orbit. Its caudal wall is the cribriform plate, perforated, separating the nasal cavity from the olfactory bulbs. This is the cranial boundary of the whole region.
- Palatine and presphenoid: caudal and ventral parts of the medial orbital wall, and the lateral wall of the caudal nasal cavity and nasopharynx.
- Zygomatic bone and arch: ventrolateral orbital rim and the lateral part of the floor.

The feline orbit is incomplete. Its lateral boundary is the orbital ligament, fibrous and occasionally ossified, between the zygomatic process of the frontal bone and the frontal process of the zygomatic bone. Its floor is not bone but the periorbita over the pterygopalatine fossa. Its medial wall, formed by the frontal, lacrimal, palatine and presphenoid, is paper-thin. That wall is the only tissue between the ethmoturbinates and the medial orbit, and it is the route by which nasal disease enters the orbit.

### 2.5 Nasal cavity

Each half is bounded medially by the septum (cartilage rostrally, vomer and perpendicular plate of the ethmoid caudally). Rostrally and in the middle, the ventral nasal concha (maxilloturbinate) fills most of the lumen with branching scrolls, and the dorsal nasal concha runs as a plate along the dorsolateral wall under the nasal bone. Caudally sit the ethmoturbinates. Air moves through the dorsal, middle, ventral and common meatuses. The nasolacrimal duct opens into the ventral meatus rostrally, and the ventral meatus leads caudally to the nasopharynx.

The walls are bone on three sides: nasal bone dorsally, maxilla laterally, hard palate ventrally. The only non-bony exits are the nostril and the nasopharynx. A space-occupying lesion inside the cavity therefore has three ways to produce external swelling: thin and bow the nasal bone and maxilla outward (slow processes), erode through them (destructive processes), or extend through the medial orbital wall into the orbit.

### 2.6 Upper teeth and their roots

Feline maxillary arcade per side: three incisors, canine (104), second premolar (106), third premolar (107), fourth premolar or carnassial (108), first molar (109). There is no upper first premolar. Roots: 104 one very long root; 106 one root (occasionally two, often fused); 107 two roots; 108 three roots (two buccal, one palatal); 109 small, two roots that are often fused.

Spatial relationships that matter here:

- 104 (canine). The root runs caudodorsally in the maxilla far beyond the gingiva. Its apex lies lateral to the rostral nasal cavity, close to the nasolacrimal duct, roughly at the level of 106 or 107. Periapical infection of 104 exits buccally through the thin plate over the apex (swelling of the lateral muzzle rostral to the eye, below the bridge), into the nasal cavity (unilateral nasal discharge, oronasal fistula), or compresses and inflames the nasolacrimal duct (overflow and medial canthus staining). Fractured canine crowns with a pinhole pulp exposure are common in cats and are routinely missed on a conscious look.
- 107. Two roots immediately below the infraorbital foramen, against the lateral wall of the mid nasal cavity.
- 108 (carnassial). Three roots beneath the infraorbital canal, the maxillary recess and the rostroventral orbit. Periapical infection of 108 exits buccally (cheek swelling below the eye), dorsally into the infraorbital tissues and periorbita (periorbital cellulitis, which narrows the fissure through pain and lid swelling), dorsomedially into the maxillary recess and nasal cavity, or caudally toward the pterygopalatine fossa and retrobulbar space (exophthalmos, third eyelid protrusion, pain on opening the mouth).
- 109. Tiny, close to the ventral orbit and the pterygopalatine fossa, a path to retrobulbar abscess.

### 2.7 Infraorbital region and nerves

The maxillary nerve enters the pterygopalatine fossa, passes through the short infraorbital canal above the roots of 108 and 107, and exits the infraorbital foramen as the infraorbital nerve supplying the muzzle, nose and upper lip. Periapical disease of 107 or 108 sits against this canal. Pain carried from it produces facial guarding and blepharospasm before any swelling appears.

### 2.8 Orbit and retrobulbar tissues

Lateral to the medial orbital wall: periorbita, globe, extraocular muscles (the medial rectus and dorsal oblique lie against the medial wall), retrobulbar fat, optic nerve, the zygomatic salivary gland ventrolaterally, and the pterygopalatine fossa ventrally with the maxillary artery and nerve. Orbital disease narrows the fissure through pain, lid swelling, or enophthalmos (painful or restrictive processes), and widens it with exophthalmos and third eyelid protrusion when something adds volume behind the globe. Globe position, retropulsion and the third eyelid are the bedside readouts.

### 2.9 Regional lymph nodes, mapped rather than assumed

There is no constant lymph node under the medial canthus or the nasal bridge in the cat. The named nodes of the head and neck:

- Mandibular lymph nodes: two or three per side, ventral to the angle of the jaw where the facial vein crosses the mandible, in the intermandibular space. Several centimeters ventral and caudal to the swelling.
- Parotid lymph node: small, at the rostral base of the ear under the parotid gland, caudal to the lateral canthus and the temporomandibular joint. Caudal to the orbit, not medial.
- Medial retropharyngeal lymph node: large and deep, dorsolateral to the pharynx, ventral to the wing of the atlas. Far caudal.
- Lateral retropharyngeal: inconstant.
- Facial (buccal) node: inconstant in the dog. I do not know of a reliable description in the cat. If present it lies rostral to the masseter near the facial vein, ventral to the zygomatic arch, not at the medial canthus.

Drainage of the region: eyelids, conjunctiva, rostral nasal cavity and the upper teeth drain to the mandibular nodes; the caudal nasal cavity and nasopharynx drain to the medial retropharyngeal node. So an enlarged node cannot be what is under this swelling. A node is relevant only as a secondary site: enlarged mandibular nodes are sampled to stage lymphoma or carcinoma, and they are a classic place to find Cryptococcus yeast.

"Lymphatic tissue" at the site itself means something different: lymphoma arising in the nasal mucosa and submucosa (the most common feline nasal tumor), or lymphoid hyperplasia of the nasal mucosa. That is an intranasal process, not a node, and it is analyzed under neoplasia.

### 2.10 Cranial boundary

Caudally: the cribriform plate and the olfactory bulbs. Dorsally: the frontal sinus and its caudal wall against the frontal lobe. Caudal to the orbit: the optic canal and the orbital fissure into the middle cranial fossa. Nasal cryptococcosis, nasal carcinoma, nasal lymphoma and sino-orbital aspergillosis all extend through the cribriform plate; retrobulbar disease can extend through the orbital fissure. Seizures, behavior change, obtundation, loss of vision or menace, or anisocoria mark that crossing.

## 3. Coronal CT reconstruction

Two slices are needed because the described swelling spans both.

### Slice A: through the right medial canthus and rostral orbit

Approximately above the carnassial (108). Set the level on the actual images.

From the dorsal midline outward and downward on the right:

1. Skin and thin subcutis over the nasofrontal junction.
2. Orbicularis oculi, levator anguli oculi medialis, the medial palpebral ligament, and the lacrimal sac in the lacrimal fossa deep to the medial canthal skin; the base of the third eyelid ventromedial to the globe.
3. Bone: the caudal nasal bone and the frontal bone dorsally; the lacrimal bone at the rostromedial rim with the lacrimal canal entering the maxilla; the frontal bone forming the dorsomedial orbital wall; dorsal to that, possibly the rostral compartment of the frontal sinus.
4. Nasal cavity: ethmoturbinates filling the lumen medially, remnants of the maxilloturbinate ventrolaterally, the bony septum at midline, the ventral meatus above the hard palate, and the maxillary recess ventrolaterally between the ethmoturbinates and the carnassial roots.
5. The medial orbital wall (frontal, lacrimal, palatine), thin.
6. Orbit: globe, medial rectus and dorsal oblique, retrobulbar fat, periorbita, the orbital ligament dorsolaterally, zygomatic arch and zygomatic salivary gland ventrolaterally.
7. Maxilla ventrolaterally: the infraorbital canal, and below it the three roots of 108 with 109 just caudal.
8. Hard palate and oral cavity ventrally.
9. Just caudal to this slice: the cribriform plate and the olfactory bulbs.

### Slice B: through the nasal bridge rostral to the eye

At the canine apex and 106 to 107.

1. Skin, minimal subcutis, levator nasolabialis, periosteum.
2. Nasal bones dorsally, the nasomaxillary suture, and the maxilla forming the lateral wall down to the alveolar margin.
3. Nasal cavity: maxilloturbinate scrolls, the dorsal concha under the nasal bone, the cartilaginous septum, the ventral meatus.
4. In the lateral wall: the nasolacrimal duct running rostroventrally under the mucosa, with the apex of the 104 root rising toward it from the alveolus; the infraorbital foramen opening laterally at or just caudal to this level; the roots of 106 and 107 ventrally.
5. Hard palate ventrally.

## 4. Candidate by candidate: the pathway each would need

### 4.1 Maxillary dental root abscess

Pathway: pulp necrosis (crown fracture, resorptive lesion, deep periodontal pocket) leads to a periapical granuloma or abscess; pressure erodes the thinnest adjacent bone; pus follows the path of least resistance.

For 104 the thinnest bone is the buccal plate over the apex and the lateral nasal wall. The output is lateral muzzle swelling rostral to the eye, unilateral nasal discharge, or duct obstruction with tear overflow. For 108 the thinnest bone is the buccal alveolar plate and the floor of the infraorbital region and maxillary recess. The output is cheek swelling below the eye, periorbital cellulitis, or retrobulbar abscess.

Fit to the described geometry: conditional. The classic centers are the lateral muzzle (104) and below the eye (108). The medial canthus and the dorsal nasal bridge are the points farthest from both. On a cat's face those distances are under a centimeter, and cellulitis tracking dorsally along the levator nasolabialis and the periosteum can reach the medial canthus. So dental disease fits if the swelling's true center of mass is rostroventral to the medial canthus (infraorbital region) and extends upward. It fits poorly if the center is the nasal bone itself with the bridge widened.

Can dental disease narrow the eye before an oral lesion is obvious? Yes, by three routes: pain carried by the infraorbital and other maxillary nerve branches (blepharospasm), periorbital cellulitis from 108 (lid swelling and enophthalmos from orbital pain), and nasolacrimal obstruction from 104 (overflow and conjunctival irritation). In cats a periapical abscess often has no gingival swelling at all, because the pus exits through the buccal plate above the mucogingival line rather than into the mouth. A tooth can look normal from the crown and have a resorbed or abscessed root.

What would make dental the answer: a swelling continuous with the alveolar bone on palpation through the lip; pain on pressure over 104 or 108; a discolored, fractured or mobile tooth; a draining tract in the buccal vestibule; periapical lucency with loss of the lamina dura on dental radiographs or CT; a tract on CT from the apex to the swelling; and no intranasal mass.

### 4.2 Nasal foreign body

Pathway for direct external swelling: there is none. A grass blade, awn or seed in a meatus displaces air, not bone. The nasal cavity's dorsal and lateral walls are rigid, and its only soft outlets are the nostril and the nasopharynx. A foreign body on its own produces sneezing, pawing, unilateral discharge and sometimes epistaxis, with a normal face.

For a foreign body to produce this surface pattern, one of these secondary events is required: migration through the lateral nasal wall into the maxilla or subcutis with a foreign-body granuloma or abscess; entry into the nasolacrimal duct or lacrimal sac with dacryocystitis (a recognized route for plant material, presenting exactly at the medial canthus); secondary bacterial osteomyelitis of the nasal bone or maxilla; or secondary fungal colonization. Each of those is then an abscess, granuloma or fungal lesion with a foreign body at its center, and the imaging and sampling plan is the one for that process.

A separate pathway is a foreign body from outside: a thorn, splinter or air-gun pellet entering through the skin at the medial canthus or bridge, or a stick entering through the mouth behind the last molar into the orbit. The skin route gives a subcutaneous or lacrimal-sac lesion with a healed puncture. The oral route gives a retrobulbar lesion with exophthalmos and pain on opening the mouth. A metallic pellet is obvious on any radiograph or CT. Plant material is often invisible on CT and is found by rhinoscopy or at surgery.

Fit: poor as a direct cause of the bulge; good as a cause of dacryocystitis at the medial canthus or of a granuloma or abscess, which are then the real lesions.

### 4.3 Bacterial abscess or cellulitis

The spaces at this site that can hold pus, with their usual origins:

1. Subcutis over the nasal bone and maxilla: bite or scratch wound (the puncture hides in the fur and seals in days), or extension from a dental or nasal source. Bone intact.
2. Lacrimal sac: dacryocystitis, from a foreign body in the sac or duct, from nasal disease obstructing the duct, or from a 104 root lesion.
3. Subperiosteal space of the maxilla or nasal bone: from a tooth root or from osteomyelitis.
4. Infraorbital and periorbital fat: from 107 or 108.
5. Medial orbit, between the medial orbital wall and the periorbita: from the ethmoidal region or the frontal sinus crossing the thin wall, or from the lacrimal sac.
6. Retrobulbar space and pterygopalatine fossa: from 108 or 109, from an oral penetrating foreign body, or hematogenous.
7. Frontal sinus: empyema from chronic rhinitis; presents on the forehead.
8. Nasal cavity: bacterial rhinitis is nearly always secondary (viral damage, foreign body, tooth, tumor, fungus) and does not by itself expand the face.

Fit: a subcutaneous abscess or early cellulitis fits the geometry well because it respects no deep boundary. A lacrimal sac abscess fits the center exactly. Periorbital cellulitis from 108 fits if the center is infraorbital. All of these would be expected to be warm, painful, often febrile, with a course of days. A firm, painless, cool swelling present for weeks is a poor fit for any of them.

### 4.4 Cryptococcosis and other fungal granulomatous disease

Cryptococcus (C. neoformans and C. gattii) enters by inhalation and establishes in the nasal cavity, where it forms a gelatinous granulomatous mass. Pathway to this surface pattern: the mass replaces turbinates, then thins and erodes the nasal bone and the dorsal maxilla, lifting the skin of the bridge (the classic "Roman nose" deformity), and spreads along the medial orbital wall into the periorbital tissues and the orbit, and along the nasolacrimal duct. Common accompaniments: sneezing and serous to bloody discharge on one or both sides, a polyp-like mass visible at the nostril, cutaneous nodules or ulcers on the bridge, enlarged mandibular nodes containing yeast, chorioretinitis or optic neuritis on fundic examination, and later neurological signs from cribriform extension. The subcutaneous component is usually firm or doughy rather than fluctuant or bone-hard; the intranasal tissue is gelatinous.

Other fungi. Sino-orbital aspergillosis (Aspergillus felis and relatives, with brachycephalic cats over-represented) begins in the caudal nasal cavity and sinuses and invades the orbit through the medial and ventral walls, producing exophthalmos, third eyelid protrusion, and ulceration of the palate or the pterygopalatine mucosa; its center is retrobulbar rather than at the medial canthus. Sporotrichosis is cutaneous and ulcerative, not subsurface. Histoplasma, Blastomyces, Coccidioides and others are regional and uncommon in the nose but exist; where the cat has lived matters.

Fit: cryptococcosis is a good geometric fit for all six constraints. It must not sit under a generic infection heading, because its test is cheap, fast and noninvasive (section 7).

### 4.5 Neoplasia

Feline nasal cavity tumors are dominated by lymphoma and carcinoma (adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, undifferentiated carcinoma), with sarcomas (fibrosarcoma, chondrosarcoma, osteosarcoma) less common. Maxillary bone tumors and oral squamous cell carcinoma of the maxilla can grow dorsally into the nasal cavity. In a white cat, actinic squamous cell carcinoma of the eyelid skin or nasal planum is a specific risk, but it is a surface lesion (crust, erosion, ulcer), and the description argues against it unless an early lesion is hidden under the staining.

Pathway to the surface: an expanding intranasal mass obstructs airflow (stertor, unilateral loss of airflow), obstructs the nasolacrimal duct (overflow, staining), destroys turbinates and the septum (epistaxis), erodes the nasal bone and maxilla (subcutaneous mass on the bridge), crosses the medial orbital wall (exophthalmos, or a narrowed fissure with globe displacement if it fills the rostromedial orbit), crosses the cribriform plate (neurological signs), and extends to the nasopharynx (snoring, dysphagia). Carcinomas are the most destructive to bone. Lymphoma more often fills and remodels than it lyses, more often involves both sides and the nasopharynx, and more often enlarges regional nodes, but the overlap is wide and imaging never settles it. Sarcomas and bone tumors produce the hardest swellings, sometimes with new bone.

Fit: carcinoma and lymphoma are good fits. The midline nose and the unilateral, localized pattern say only that the process has not yet reached the rostral cavity or the other side. Nothing in the description separates them from cryptococcosis.

### 4.6 Orbital disease

Primary orbital processes (abscess, cellulitis, neoplasia, feline restrictive orbital myofibroblastic sarcoma) sit lateral to the medial orbital wall. They produce exophthalmos with third eyelid protrusion when they add volume behind the globe, or enophthalmos with a narrowed fissure when they are painful or restrictive. Their swelling is at the orbital rim, strongest where the process is. A rostromedial orbital lesion would fit the medial canthus but would be expected to displace the globe laterally or forward and to resist retropulsion. The restrictive myofibroblastic sarcoma is named because it is specific to cats: progressive, firm, infiltrative periorbital thickening, a globe that cannot be retropulsed, lids that lose mobility, no discrete mass on CT and little bone change, eventually crossing the bridge to the other orbit.

Fit: conditional. It depends entirely on globe position, retropulsion, third eyelid and pupil, none of which the description reports.

### 4.7 Enlarged lymph node

Poor fit on anatomy alone. No node lies there. See 2.9.

### 4.8 Inflammatory rhinitis

Chronic lymphoplasmacytic or post-viral rhinitis destroys turbinates but does not add volume and does not deform the face. It belongs on the CT differential as the explanation for turbinate loss and fluid without a mass. It is a poor fit for the described external bulge.

### 4.9 Dacryocystitis as its own entry

An infected or obstructed lacrimal sac produces a swelling centered at the medial canthus, slightly ventral, with mucopurulent or stained discharge, conjunctivitis on that side, and sometimes a fistula through the skin below the canthus. In cats it is uncommon and usually secondary (plant foreign body, 104 root disease, nasal mass). It fits the described center and the staining precisely. It does not by itself explain a widened nasal bridge. Pressing on the sac and watching the puncta is a one-second test.

## 5. Geometric fit summary

No probabilities. This is only which anatomy can produce the described pattern.

Good fit for the pattern as described (center at medial canthus and bridge, subsurface, unilateral, overflow):

- An intranasal expansile or destructive process erupting dorsolaterally: cryptococcoma, carcinoma, lymphoma, sarcoma.
- Subcutaneous cellulitis or abscess over the bridge (bite wound or extension), especially if acute, warm and painful.
- Dacryocystitis or lacrimal sac abscess, especially if the center is just ventral to the canthus and the sac expresses material.

Conditional fit (depends on an observation not yet made):

- Periapical disease of 108 or 107 with dorsal tracking: fits if the center is infraorbital and the swelling is continuous with the alveolar bone.
- Periapical disease of 104: fits the overflow and staining and muzzle swelling rostral to the eye; fits the medial canthus itself poorly.
- Rostromedial orbital disease: fits if the globe is displaced or cannot be retropulsed.
- Sino-orbital aspergillosis: fits if there is exophthalmos and palatal or pterygopalatine ulceration.

Poor fit:

- Intranasal foreign body as a direct cause.
- Enlarged lymph node of any named group.
- Uncomplicated rhinitis.
- Frontal sinus disease without rostral extension.
- A surface skin tumor, unless the staining hides an erosion.

## 6. Observations that separate the candidates before any imaging

Each is a bedside act. Together they take a few minutes.

1. Texture and temperature of the swelling: bone-hard (tumor, new bone), firm-rubbery (granuloma, lymphoma, sarcoma), doughy (cryptococcoma), fluctuant and warm (abscess). Painful or not.
2. Dorsal view: is the bridge wider on the right? That is the nasal cavity speaking.
3. Airflow per nostril (a cold glass slide or a wisp of cotton): loss on the right localizes an obstructing mass or discharge.
4. Nostril inspection: discharge and its character (serous, mucopurulent, bloody) and any tissue at the nostril.
5. Lift the lip: look at 104, 107 and 108 for fracture, discoloration, gingival swelling, a vestibular tract, or mobility, and press on each. Look at the palate. Look behind the last molar.
6. Press on the lacrimal sac and watch the puncta.
7. Wipe the dark material away and watch what re-accumulates over an hour: tears, mucopurulent material, or blood.
8. Fluorescein: corneal uptake (an ulcer explains the squint on its own), and whether dye reaches the right nostril within several minutes. In cats a negative passage is not reliable on its own; a flush under anesthesia is the definitive patency test.
9. Globe: position (forward, back, deviated), retropulsion compared with the left, third eyelid position, pupil size in light and dark. Miosis with ptosis and enophthalmos is Horner's syndrome, which makes the narrowing a neurological sign rather than a mechanical one, with its own list of causes.
10. Fundus: chorioretinitis or optic neuritis is a systemic fungal flag.
11. Mandibular nodes: size and symmetry.
12. Temperature, appetite, weight, sneezing, snoring, pawing at the face, head shaking, halitosis, dropping food.
13. History: onset and tempo; outdoor access and fights; region of residence and travel; FeLV and FIV status; age; breed and skull shape.

## 7. Diagnostic workup, designed for discrimination

### 7.1 Same day, awake, cheap

- Serum cryptococcal antigen (latex agglutination or lateral flow). High sensitivity and specificity in cats with nasal disease. A positive effectively answers the question. A negative makes cryptococcosis unlikely but does not exclude a small localized lesion. This is the single highest-yield test for this geometry and should precede anything invasive.
- Fine needle aspiration of the swelling through clipped skin, directed away from the globe, with cytology: pus and bacteria (abscess); encapsulated yeast with narrow-based budding (Cryptococcus, also visible with India ink); a monomorphic round-cell population (lymphoma); cohesive epithelial cells (carcinoma); mixed inflammation with no organism (granuloma, foreign-body reaction, or a sampling miss). Submit any purulent aspirate for aerobic and anaerobic culture.
- Fine needle aspiration of any enlarged mandibular node.
- CBC, chemistry, FeLV and FIV status.

### 7.2 One anesthetic event, in this order

Order matters. Imaging comes first because biopsy and rhinoscopy bleed, and the blood obscures the CT.

1. Skull CT, thin slices (1 mm or less), bone and soft tissue algorithms, before and after intravenous contrast. Field: the whole skull including both dental arcades, both orbits, the frontal sinuses, the cribriform plate, the nasopharynx, the tympanic bullae, and the mandibular and retropharyngeal nodes. If the lacrimal sac is implicated, inject contrast through a punctum (CT dacryocystography) to map the duct.
2. Full oral examination with periodontal probing and intraoral dental radiographs of 104 through 109 on the right, and the left for comparison. Dental films resolve periapical lucency and root resorption better than CT; CT shows the tract. They are complementary.
3. Rhinoscopy, anterograde through the nostril with a rigid 2.7 mm scope and retrograde around the soft palate with a flexible scope: direct view of a mass, a foreign body, fungal plaques, turbinate loss; removal of a foreign body; a target for biopsy.
4. Biopsy of any intranasal or subcutaneous mass, with multiple deep samples. Superficial samples of a nasal lymphoma or carcinoma commonly return "lymphoplasmacytic rhinitis" because the overlying mucosa is inflamed. Do not pass an instrument through the nostril further than the distance from the nostril to the medial canthus, to stay rostral to the cribriform plate.
5. Tissue, not swabs, for aerobic and anaerobic bacterial culture and for fungal culture. Surface swabs of nasal discharge grow normal flora and answer nothing.
6. Histopathology with fungal stains (PAS, GMS, mucicarmine for the cryptococcal capsule) and, if round cells, immunohistochemistry for lymphoma phenotype (CD3, CD79a or PAX5), with clonality testing (PARR) if morphology is equivocal.
7. Nasolacrimal flush under the same anesthetic. If the duct is blocked, the dacryocystogram shows where.
8. If a tooth shows periapical disease: extract it, and still submit the soft tissue at the apex and the lining of any tract for histopathology, so that a tumor behind a tooth is not missed.

### 7.3 What the CT would show for each candidate

| Candidate | Bone | Soft tissue | Contrast | Other |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Odontogenic abscess (104, 107, 108) | Periapical lucency, loss of lamina dura, widened periodontal space, cortical defect of the buccal plate, sometimes periosteal new bone; turbinates intact or focally involved beside the apex | Tract of fluid or gas from the apex to the subcutis, infraorbital region or nasal cavity | Rim enhancement around a fluid center | Root resorption or fracture visible; no intranasal mass |

| Nasal or maxillary carcinoma | Aggressive, moth-eaten lysis of turbinates, septum, nasal bone, maxilla and orbital wall; cribriform lysis | Unilateral heterogeneous mass with subcutaneous and orbital extension | Heterogeneous | Nodes enlarged in a minority |
| Orbital primary | Orbital rim or wall lysis if malignant; none in restrictive myofibroblastic sarcoma | Retrobulbar or periorbital mass; globe displaced | Variable | Nasal cavity normal |

Reading bone change. Smooth expansion with a thinned but continuous cortex means slow growth from inside (a cyst, a benign tumor, a long-standing granuloma). Moth-eaten or permeative lysis with a cortical breach and a soft-tissue mass means an aggressive process, which is malignancy or invasive infection (fungal or bacterial osteomyelitis). Lysis with periosteal new bone leans toward infection, including dental. Destruction of the septum and the cribriform plate leans toward carcinoma, aspergillosis, or advanced lymphoma or cryptococcosis. None of these patterns is specific enough to replace tissue. CT's jobs are to name the compartment, show the extent, pick the biopsy target, and stage.

Contrast is useful. It separates a fluid-filled abscess with an enhancing rim from a solid enhancing mass, shows intraorbital and intracranial extension, and characterizes nodes. Dental roots are read on the pre-contrast bone window and on intraoral films.

### 7.4 When each test actually answers the question

- Cryptococcal antigen: answers cryptococcosis, yes or no, within a day.
- FNA cytology of the swelling: can answer abscess, Cryptococcus, lymphoma, and sometimes carcinoma; it excludes nothing when it returns inflammation only.
- CT: answers the compartment and the extent; separates a dental tract from an intranasal mass from an orbital mass from a subcutaneous pocket; does not separate a fungal mass from a tumor.
- Dental radiographs and oral examination under anesthesia: answer odontogenic, yes or no.
- Rhinoscopy: answers foreign body and gives the biopsy its target.
- Biopsy with histopathology, special stains and immunohistochemistry: answers tumor type and fungal identity.
- Tissue culture: answers the bacterial species for a true abscess and the fungal species when histology shows hyphae or yeast.
- Lymph node aspirate: stages lymphoma and carcinoma and finds Cryptococcus; it never explains the facial swelling itself.
- Nasolacrimal flush and dacryocystogram: answer dacryocystitis and locate a duct obstruction.
- MRI: only if CT shows cribriform or orbital apex involvement, or the cat develops neurological signs.

### 7.5 Shortest path

Day one, awake: the hands-on examination from section 6, serum cryptococcal antigen, FNA of the swelling with cytology and culture, node FNA if a node is enlarged, bloodwork with retroviral status. This can close the question for cryptococcosis, bite-wound abscess and often lymphoma without an anesthetic.

If day one is unrevealing, or returns a solid or uncertain lesion: one anesthetic with CT first, then dental radiographs and oral examination, then rhinoscopy and biopsy, then nasolacrimal flush, with tissue to pathology and culture. That sequence addresses every compartment in section 3 in one event.

## 8. Uncertainty notes

- The feline canine root apex and the nasolacrimal duct are closely related, but how closely varies between cats and skull shapes. A dacryocystogram on the actual cat is the only way to see it.
- I am not certain a constant facial (buccal) lymph node exists in the cat. The mapping treats it as absent or inconstant, which does not change the conclusion that no node lies at the medial canthus.
- The exact rostral extent of the feline frontal sinus relative to the medial canthus varies with skull shape; it is reported here as near the orbit.
- The position of the infraorbital foramen is given as roughly dorsal to the third premolar; sources place it between the third and fourth premolars depending on skull shape.
- The dental landmark assigned to each coronal slice (108 for the medial canthus slice, the canine apex for the bridge slice) is an approximation for a mesocephalic cat and should be set on the actual images.
- Aspergillus serology and galactomannan testing in cats are less validated than the cryptococcal antigen test and are secondary.
- Nothing in this document is a diagnosis of the photographed cat. Texture, tempo and the eye examination will move the ranking more than any further reasoning from the description.
